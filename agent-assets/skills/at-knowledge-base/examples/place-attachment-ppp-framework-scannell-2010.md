# Defining Place Attachment: The Person–Process–Place (PPP) Framework (Scannell & Gifford, 2010)

## Summary

Place attachment, studied across disaster psychology, immigration, park planning and pro-environmental behavior, has accumulated widely varying definitions with no agreed general one. Scannell and Gifford review these definitions and synthesize them into a proposed three-dimensional person–process–place (PPP) organizing framework: who is attached (individual or collective), how the attachment is manifested (affect, cognition, behavior), and what it is attached to (spatial level, specificity, social or physical elements). They also review functions of place attachment, evidence for multidimensionality, and PPP's uses.

## Aims

To explore "the commonalities across the different permutations of the concept" so as to "shape, then structure, a coherent understanding of it": to propose a three-dimensional framework "that usefully structures the varied definitions in the literature," and to review potential functions of place attachment.

## Key Concepts

- **Place attachment** — "the bonding that occurs between individuals and their meaningful environments." Under PPP: "a bond between an individual or group and a place that can vary in terms of spatial level, degree of specificity, and social or physical features of the place, and is manifested through affective, cognitive, and behavioral psychological processes."
- **Place identity** — Proshansky's "physical world socialization of the self": self-definitions derived from places, incorporating memories, values, preferences and categorizations about the physical environment.
- **Place dependence** — valuing a place for the activities and goal-relevant resources it supports (Stokols & Shumaker, 1981); "generic place dependence" applies to a type of place.
- **Community of place** — social ties rooted in a location, as distinct from communities of interest, which need not be place-bound; only the former is specific to place attachment.
- **Territoriality vs. attachment** — territoriality rests on ownership, control and access regulation (marking, defense); attachment is an affective, proximity-maintaining bond that need not involve control.

## Main Content

### Framework — the PPP tripartite framework (proposed)

Place attachment is multidimensional (Fig. 1): the **person** (who is attached, on individually or collectively held meanings), the **psychological process** (how affect, cognition and behavior are manifested), and the **place** (what the attachment is to and its characteristics). Each dimension has levels that "may overlap, but may also be separable." The tapestry tying a person to a place "is unique for each individual."

### Framework dimension — Person: individual and collective attachment

Definitions tend to emphasize one level, but the two overlap. **Individual**: connections to places evoking memories and personal growth; for Manzo (2005), "experience-in-place" creates meaning. **Group**: shared symbolic meanings (Low, 1992) across cultures, genders and religions.

[Example] Forests were perceived as more threatening by Hispanic Americans, African Americans and women than by European Americans and men (Virden & Walker, 1999), attributed to transmitted group meanings.

Cultural meanings shape individual attachment; positive individual experiences can strengthen cultural attachment.

### Framework dimension — Psychological process: affect, cognition, behavior

Organized like attitudes and prejudice in social psychology.

- **Affect** — emotional bonds: Tuan's "topophilia," pride and well-being; displacement grief supports this (Fried, 1963; Fullilove, 1996). Bonds can be negative or ambivalent, but attachment is usually defined positively.
- **Cognition** — memories, beliefs, meanings and knowledge that connect places to the self: familiarity (Fullilove), place schemas such as settlement identity (Feldman, 1990), and place identity, including "place-related distinctiveness" (e.g., "we are 'island people'").
- **Behavior** — proximity-maintaining actions (Hidalgo & Hernández, 2001: the bond's "main characteristic … is to maintain closeness"), linked to length of residence, efforts to return, homesickness and pilgrimage. Attached people also leave regularly, and rigid bonds can become dysfunctional. Further behaviors: place reconstruction and relocation to similar places.

[Example] After a 1974 tornado, Xenia, Ohio, residents overrode new zoning plans and rebuilt the town much as it had been (Francaviglia, 1978).

### Framework dimension — Place: spatial level, specificity, social and physical

Called "perhaps the most important dimension." Attachment varies by scale; Hidalgo and Hernández (2001) found stronger attachment at home and city than neighborhood levels, and social stronger than physical.

- **Social** — the main research focus; urban sociologists hold attachment is necessarily social, sometimes conflating it with sense of community. Social attachment centers on the place as an arena for interaction or a symbol of one's group (civic attachment; nationalism).
- **Physical** — rests on features of built and natural settings (place dependence; environmental identity, Clayton, 2003). Specificity matters: specific-place attachment was linked to unwillingness to substitute, wilderness-class attachment to visiting other areas (Williams et al., 1992). In Stedman's (2003) meaning-mediated model, people attach to the meanings that physical features symbolize and constrain.

### Functions — why place attachment exists

Each proposed function implies a definition locatable in PPP:

- **Survival and security** — staying near and knowing resources (physical, behavioral, cognitive, individual). Following Bowlby, Fried (2000) argued proximity gives security that allows exploration.
- **Goal support and self-regulation** — attachment to goal-supporting places (place dependence), or to favorite places that are restorative (Korpela, 1989).
- **Continuity** — "place-congruent" (places matching one's values) and "place-referent" (places linking to one's past) continuity.
- **Other** — belongingness, identity and self-esteem, a precursor to place friendship in children.

### Evidence review — multidimensionality

Validation requires showing components behave as proposed, by an instrument's factors or by differing effects. Residents attached to specific areas of a Norwegian municipality opposed a hydropower plant while those attached to the whole municipality favored it (Vorkinn & Riese, 2001); on the Appalachian Trail, place identity was linked to rating problems as more important and place dependence to less (Kyle et al., 2004). "Few such studies exist."

### Comparison — PPP versus earlier frameworks

Earlier definitions "represent pieces of the whole" (e.g., Fullilove's is process-only; place identity lacks place aspects and behavior). Shumaker and Taylor (1983) is residential and affect-focused; PPP adds cognition (memories, schemas, meaning, knowledge) and divides social into social-symbolic and social-interaction levels and physical into natural and built. Low and Altman (1992) treat levels as inseparable, PPP as related yet distinct, and PPP rests on newer studies.

### Uses — of the PPP framework

- **Organization** — a coherent structure and teaching tool.
- **Definitional and methodological** — specify a study's emphasis on each dimension; build instruments with items per process level, or interview categories per dimension.
- **Heuristic** — following Blalock, explore frequencies of level combinations toward a typology; compile inventories of causes and effects.
- **Applied** — PPP-based open-ended questions to surface groups' place meanings in planning conflicts.

Definitions should state their emphasis within each dimension.

## Future Directions

- Functions per dimension: group-level goals; whether bonds at different spatial levels meet different needs.
- Validate multidimensionality via an instrument's factor structure and unique effects of levels.
- Which level combinations are most common for which individuals, groups or places; how individually vs. collectively based attachment differ.
- Predictors (personality, culture, environment); determinants of physical vs. social attachment.
