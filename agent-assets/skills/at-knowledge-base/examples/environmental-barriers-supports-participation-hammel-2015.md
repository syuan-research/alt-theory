## Summary

Environment is central to disability participation, yet the ICF taxonomy of environmental factors does not show how factors relate, and measuring them in a consumer-responsive way is difficult. The paper pools qualitative data from 36 focus groups (201 people with disabilities, mostly spinal cord injury, traumatic brain injury and stroke) from five projects and analyzes them by constant comparison. It proposes a conceptual framework of eight environmental domains acting at micro, mesa and macro levels. The data confirmed some ICF categories and brought forward others (information and technology access, economic circumstances). The framework later served as the basis for writing items to measure environmental barriers and supports.

## Objective

"To describe environmental factors that influence participation of people with disabilities"; more specifically, to develop a "theoretically grounded, conceptual framework of the relations between environmental factors and participation, grounded in the first-hand experiences of people with disabilities".

## Key Concepts

- **Participation**: everyday involvement in home, community, work, economic and social life; its choice, control and engagement are what environments enable or disable.
- **Facilitator / barrier**: environmental features that enhance or inhibit participation, depending on the person's social role and context; the same feature can be both.
- **Insider perspective**: environmental influence as reported by participants from first-hand experience, not an objective audit of environmental features.
- **Micro / mesa / macro levels (proposed use)**: personal or immediate interactions (micro), neighborhood or community (mesa), societal (macro); the authors state they correspond to the Social Determinants of Health framework (WHO, CDC).
- **Participation fulcrum**: in the paper's Figure 3, participation choice and control can swing between enfranchisement and disenfranchisement depending on "environmental press".

## Study — pooled secondary analysis of focus-group data

[Context: disability, rehabilitation; home, community, work and social participation]

### Method — data, participants and procedure

- Secondary analysis of qualitative data from five projects (Table 1: four funded programs; data sources in Illinois, Wisconsin, Ohio, Colorado, DC, New Jersey, Michigan, Washington, Missouri): 12 groups with 63 people (primarily SCI, TBI, stroke); 12 with 65 with SCI; 7 with 33 with TBI; 5 with 40 with SCI, cerebral palsy, MS, stroke, postpolio. Total 36 groups, 201 participants from 8 states.
- Purposive maximum-variation sampling in each project; participants reported many coexisting disabilities (psychiatric, cognitive, neuromuscular, sensory, chronic conditions).
- Groups were cofacilitated by researchers and people with disabilities; community stakeholders helped write questions, interpret data and conduct member checks. Groups asked open-ended questions on what participation means, how it is experienced, and barriers and facilitators.

### Method — analysis (grounded theory, constant comparison)

Descriptive coding within projects with member checking; themes compiled by two lead qualitative researchers into a coding/member-checking template (themes, representative language, definitions); team review and revision of ambiguous themes; triangulation across groups; development of a conceptual framework organizing themes into domains.

### Results — eight environmental domains (framework proposed by this paper)

Four with more similarities than differences across disabilities:
- **Information and technology (IT) access**: usability of information and delivery technology (phones, computers, internet).
- **Social support and societal attitudes**: informational, instrumental and emotional support, networks; marginalization or inclusion. Differences between visible (wheelchair) and hidden (cognitive, communication) disabilities.
- **Systems and policies**: health care, housing, employment, transportation, legal and other systems.
- **Economic**: adequacy of resources to live and participate, plus societal economic issues.

Four where detailed features differ by functional need (mobility, cognitive, social/communication, sensory) rather than diagnosis:
- **Built environment**: architecture, sidewalks, stations, noise, crowds, air quality, safety, lighting.
- **Natural environment**: weather, climate, urban/rural, topography.
- **Assistive technology**: mobility devices, daily-living equipment, home modifications, communication technology.
- **Transportation**: private and public modes; affordability, availability and quality similar across groups.

[Example] Stroke survivor: strangers stare, a waiter addresses the children instead of the participant, speech difficulty read as drunkenness; "problems in the world around me, not inside my body".

### Results — relations among factors

- Factors cross-cut and accumulate: snow and rain (natural) interact with snow-removal services (systems) and bus-shelter design (built). One participant described participation as "one big, messy complex system", spanning ramps, weather, transport, attendant hours and benefits.
- Barriers vary by setting and transitions between settings (an accessible home vs inaccessible places outside; "ADA accessible" venues that were not).
- Economic factors were consistently critical, at system level (benefit cut-offs deterring work) and immediate level (rent, medication, money for socializing).

[Example] Wheelchair user planning an outing: ramp safety depends on ice, shoveling, transport reliability, attendant hours and benefit continuity.

[Example] A Social Security work-disincentive quote (benefits cut above $12,000 a year).

### Framework — transactional model across micro, mesa and macro levels

Each domain has micro, mesa and macro factors (Figure 2). Positive influence yields choice, control and resiliency (micro), social capital and community membership (mesa), and societal enfranchisement and empowerment (macro); negative influence yields disengagement, isolation, segregation, disenfranchisement and discrimination. Under this framework, the paper says action on barriers at any level can change participation across levels. Social supports at all three levels are the worked example (Figure 3): access to peer support such as a Center for Independent Living may override individual, community and societal barriers, while lack of awareness of such communities leads to isolation.

[Example] A participant referred through a Center for Independent Living left a nursing home for their own place.

### Interpretations (selected)

- The ICF taxonomy was largely reproduced but "belies the complex relations" the groups described.
- Macro-level factors (economics, policies, attitudes, IT access) were consistently raised across disability types.
- Disability-type differences: mobility impairments more affected by built features (for example nonadjustable examination tables); visual and cognitive impairments by inaccessible information and attitudes.

### Limitations (selected)

The five projects used different question guides and facilitators and were not all focused on the environment; physical and cognitive disabilities were inclusion criteria, so findings may not describe all disabilities; not all geographic, climate or built settings were sampled; findings reflect insider experience, not objective audits.

## Future Directions

Refine person-environment frameworks; examine the relative influence of factors on participation, health and quality of life; study environmental interventions and systems change; further qualitative and quantitative work with diverse disabilities; build consumer-focused environment assessments for evaluating interventions and policies.
