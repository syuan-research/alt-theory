# Restoration in Nature: Beyond the Conventional Narrative (Hartig)

## Summary

Hartig argues that the restoration perspective is underused in nature-and-health research, which follows a "conventional theoretical narrative" built around stress recovery theory (SRT) and attention restoration theory (ART), both individual-level, neglecting restorative processes involving multiple people. In this theory chapter (67th Nebraska Symposium on Motivation; forthcoming in the source) he sets out the restoration perspective's premises, organizes SRT and ART components into a general framework for restorative environments theory, adds level of analysis, and proposes two new theories: relational restoration theory (RRT) for resources held in closer relationships and collective restoration theory (CRT) for social resources held in a population. He closes by arguing that the narrative's evolutionary assumptions reinforce an anti-urban bias, and lays foundations for a more encompassing narrative.

## Aims

As stated in the chapter:

- set out the basic premises of the restoration perspective and why it has particular relevance for the salutary values assigned to nature experience;
- discuss the conventional theoretical narrative and organize some of its components in a general framework for restorative environments theory;
- extend the framework with two additional theories about restoration of resources held within closer relationships and held collectively by members of a population;
- consider ways to work with the framework and further develop the narrative about nature, restoration and health.

## Key Concepts

- **Restoration perspective** — one of three perspectives on adaptation (with stress and coping). Theoretical premise: people with ample protection from demands and ample coping resources "still need periodic restoration," because resources held by or between individuals get depleted in everyday activity. Practical premise: enhance opportunities to restore more easily, quickly and completely.
- **Restorative environment** — one that *promotes*, not merely *permits*, restoration. Permitting: distance from the demands that caused the need and no new demands on the depleted resource. Promoting: features and affordances that draw thoughts from demands and hold attention.
- **Theory / theoretical narrative** — following Ruonavaara (2018), theory is an "unsettled" discourse of linked concepts and propositions; a narrative is a structured way of telling about a body of theory.
- **Conventional narrative** — the "two theories" (SRT + ART) account repeated across research, textbooks and media, in many variants.
- **Restorative environments theory** — a body of theory with multiple formulations, including but not limited to SRT and ART.
- **Relational resources** (proposed) — interpersonal aspects of relationships such as trust, love, respect, mutual understanding, tolerance, shared goals and a valued shared history; they "do not exist in one person alone." Together: the **pool of relational resources**.
- **Standing arrangements** (proposed) — deliberate arrangements for supportive exchange (e.g., residential location) expected to hold over long periods and many situations.
- **Social resources** — resources in relationships among people who may not know each other but share weak bonds (community, customs, civic duties); social capital.

## Main Content

### Framework — stress, coping and restoration perspectives on adaptation

Within Saegert and Winkel's adaptive paradigm, stress research studies demands; coping research, the resources used to meet them; restoration research, how depleted resources are restored and which environmental experiences support it (Table 1).

### Argument — the "built-in" relevance of restoration for nature experience

With urbanization, "nature" came to be seen less as a workplace and more as a setting for recreation and restoration, while "urban" became grounded in everyday demands; recurrent movements between settings ("getting out of town for the weekend") link conceptions of nature probabilistically to restoration. Hartig does not claim the opposition describes reality fully. The "nature" at issue is its place in a social ecology of settings, not only measurable attributes.

### Concept — the conventional narrative and its variants

SRT and ART inspired early experiments in which vegetated settings served restoration better than urban ones, supporting the first epidemiological greenspace studies. Variants arise as the authors revised their accounts and others represented the theories for different purposes.

[Example] A WHO (2016) report says both theories are "based on the biophilia hypothesis." Hartig calls this an error: early articulations predate Wilson's biophilia essay, and Kaplan and Ulrich confirmed it lacked that influence.

### Framework — general framework for restorative environments theory (proposed)

SRT and ART components are set into a comparative framework (Table 2), without evaluating their evidence:

- **Resource** — psychophysiological (SRT) and cognitive/directed attention (ART); others, such as social support, could ground new theories.
- **Antecedent condition** — stress (SRT), mental fatigue (ART), or another depletion such as lost access to support.
- **Environmental requirements** — SRT: absence of threat (permitting); gross structure, moderate depth and complexity, a focal point and survival-serving natural contents evoking positive affect and non-vigilant attention (promoting). ART: being away and compatibility (more than threat), extent, soft fascination.
- **Outcomes** — SRT: positive affect, reduced physiological activation; ART: performance on directed-attention and executive tasks.
- **Matters of time** — duration dominates; emergence, persistence and restoration time relative to depletion time are related; frequency, periodicity and distribution across occasions "have not received systematic attention."

Under this framework, each row tells a classic plot — equilibrium, imbalance (depletion), new equilibrium (restoration) — binding stress and coping concerns to restoration.

### Framework extension — level of analysis

Level of analysis is added as a component: individual (SRT, ART), dyad/small group (RRT), population (CRT). The accounts are incomplete by design but suffice to make RRT and CRT distinct theories with hypotheses not derivable from SRT, ART or each other.

### Theory — Relational restoration theory (RRT, proposed)

[Context: closer relationships — partners, family, friends, co-workers, neighbors]

- **Resource** — the ability to rely on a close other for instrumental and emotional support, resting on the other's functional resources, standing arrangements, and most fundamentally relational resources, which build progressively so that sustained reciprocal support deepens the pool.
- **Antecedent condition** — support fails through the other's stress or fatigue, weak arrangements, or depleted relational resources (lost trust or love, unjustified non-reciprocity, abandoned shared goals); stable circumstances can make these cascade across life domains. RRT assumes relational resources have superordinate significance and the pool is "depleted but not emptied": those involved want to ease the strain and can act, including by changing arrangements.
- **Across situations** — RRT situates SRT/ART-type transactions within ongoing supportive exchange, with depletion and restoration flowing both ways between personal and relational levels. Three integral aspects of standing arrangements shape outcomes: *privacy regulation* (Altman, 1975; time alone or together can bring personal restoration and care for relationships into congruence or conflict); *reciprocity* (judged across situations; justified failures tolerated, routine unjustified ones erode relational resources); and *experiential interdependencies* (memories and anticipations, such as of favorite places, that color restoration even when alone).
- **Within situations** — person–person transactions shape person–environment transactions and vice versa. In Staats and Hartig (2004), participants judged restoration likelihood for a forest or city walk, alone or with a friend, fatigued or alert; perceived safety mediated a positive effect of company only for the forest, and with safety guaranteed being alone looked more restorative. Company may enable restoration and also enhance or degrade it.

[Example] A couple searching an unfamiliar forest for a long-sought orchid: distance from work frees resources to attend to each other; solitude eases discussion of neglected plans; shared discovery intensifies engagement; they reaffirm trust and create new memories. Hartig calls this speculative and uniformly positive, yet plausible.

- **Outcomes** — change in the pool or a specific relational resource, proximal or distal, measured at individual and dyad/group levels. Related findings: recreation motives of being with close others, family cohesion through outdoor recreation, a review of 235 wildland recreation studies reporting restorative and pro-social outcomes, and serial mediation from greenspace through perceived restorativeness to social cohesion to mental health. Research has "yet to address the assumptions and claims of RRT as such."
- **Matters of time** — how past and anticipated situations shape the present, and how long people can be together or apart before moving on, shaping the frequency and duration of restorative interludes.

RRT shows that personal restoration "does not occur in a social vacuum, even when a person is alone" — a limitation of SRT and ART.

### Theory — Collective restoration theory (CRT, proposed)

[Context: populations; stable circumstances set by customs and laws]

- **Level of analysis** — depletion and restoration spread to others; population customs and laws constrain groups' standing arrangements.
- **Resource** — support predicated on relational resources plus broadly held social resources among people known and unknown to one another.
- **Antecedent condition** — declining civic engagement (Putnam, 2000), circumstances preventing satisfactory arrangements, destruction of a shared environment or displacement, and events shaking trust in institutions (communal bereavement after assassinations, terrorist attacks, preventable disasters).
- **Restorative provisions** — institutional provisions for population access to restorative settings: time away from work (Sabbaths; national vacation legislation), social norms for taking it, availability of settings (public parks, road access), and their restorative quality (noise, conflicts, carrying capacity). Some settings restore because they are crowded with celebrants, opening contagion of mood and renewing communal identity.
- **Outcomes** — population-level measures. Catalano et al. (2020) treated collective optimism as a social resource; in Sweden, monthly suicides among reproductive-age women and male twin births covaried as expected, 1973–2016. Hartig et al. (2013), the initial test of CRT, found in Swedish monthly time series that vacationing was negatively associated with antidepressant dispensation to adults, including retirees — read as benefits spreading beyond the workforce; prescribers' vacations did not explain it, other alternatives could not be addressed.
- **Matters of time** — CRT takes "particular interest in the social regulation of time," which tracks predictable natural variation: Swedish law concentrates up to four vacation weeks in summer, justified by summer's suitability for outdoor restoration. Restorative settings are located "not only in space but also in time."

CRT complements SRT and ART, which leave implicit when people are in a setting and why they leave, and links them to RRT through the stable circumstances standing arrangements must adapt to.

### Argument — restoration in nature as a social ecological phenomenon

The framework's columns relate stress, coping and restoration; its rows nest the adaptive paradigm within the opportunity structure (RRT) and sociocultural (CRT) paradigms — a social ecological model in which individual cycles correspond to activity cycles within arrangements sensitive to societal circumstances, with influence flowing both ways (excess demands feeding national parks and vacation laws). Neglecting this ecology risks treating psychological restoration as independent of social pathways and missing barriers to nature contact — new parks may frustrate the time-poor or raise property prices.

### Argument — evolutionary assumptions and an alternative narrative

SRT and ART assume humans are adapted to early natural environments and maladapted to urban ones, portraying cities as malign and nature as benign. RRT and CRT instead emphasize selection for cooperation and continuing biological, social and cultural evolution: harmful urban conditions are consequences of particular choices, and the necessary urban feature is many people living and cooperating together, so the natural/urban distinction may weaken. Applying Gould's (1997) rule of thumb — be doubly suspicious of claims that find in nature what makes us feel good — Hartig warns that evolutionary arguments can defend cultural biases, here an anti-urban one, and proposes a problem description that does not oppose urbanization and nature preservation.

### Argument — benefits and risks of a conventional narrative

A conventional narrative aids communication but risks loyalty to one theory, perpetuated misunderstandings and path dependency. Hartig calls for a more encompassing narrative that respects SRT and ART while acknowledging their limits.

## Future Directions

- Critical assessments of SRT/ART evidence; alternative accounts such as perceptual fluency (Joye, 2007).
- RRT: experiments inducing tension, stress and fatigue in friend pairs, then comparing a shared greenhouse with a bare room and testing for greater forgiveness; interventions changing standing arrangements; surveys of how family members permit each other's restorative withdrawal, related to trust and marital stability.
- CRT: what causes collective optimism to fall and recover; where people spend vacations.
- Frequency, periodicity and distribution of restorative occasions.
- Evolutionary assumptions as a framework component, critically assessed for all theories; days and seasons as neglected natural features.
