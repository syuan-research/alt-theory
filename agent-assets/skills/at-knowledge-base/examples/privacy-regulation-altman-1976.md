# Privacy regulation theory: privacy as interpersonal boundary control

## Summary

Many definitions treat privacy as seclusion and withdrawal. Altman, reviewing earlier approaches, proposes a framework treating privacy as a dialectic, nonmonotonic, bidirectional process of interpersonal boundary control; describes the behavioral mechanisms that regulate it, its dynamics and costs; and proposes three functions, with self-identity the ultimate one. A theoretical analysis adapted from Altman's 1975 book; no new data.

## Aims

The paper "presents a theoretical analysis of the concept of privacy which emphasizes its role as an interpersonal boundary control process", and also "analyzes mechanisms and dynamics of privacy" and proposes "several functions of privacy".

## Key Concepts

### Privacy

"Selective control of access to the self or to one's group." It admits various social units, bidirectionality, and active regulation; it departs from exclusion-based definitions (Bates, Chapin, Jourard) and aligns with control-based ones (Westin, Rapoport, Proshansky et al.).

### Desired and achieved privacy

Desired privacy is "a subjectively defined ideal state" of social interaction; achieved privacy is the actual outcome of social input and output.

### Boundary

A flexible barrier between self and nonself whose permeability shifts with circumstances, like a cell membrane; it has both a "keep out" and an "open up" component.

### Crowding and isolation

The two failure states: crowding or intrusion (achieved < desired) and isolation, loneliness, boredom (achieved > desired).

- Proshansky, H. M., Ittelson, W. H., & Rivlin, L. G. (Eds.). (1970). *Environmental psychology*. Holt, Rinehart and Winston. — Privacy as control and freedom of choice, territoriality as its mechanism; Altman adopts both.

## Main Content

### Framework — properties of privacy

Under this framework, privacy has these features:

- **Units**: privacy links persons and groups in any combination.
- **Dialectic**: interaction is a continuing interplay between forces toward and away from others; privacy is a changing, momentary ideal level of contact. This is "at variance" with withdrawal-only views (Milgram, Schwartz, Jourard, Westin's states).
- **Nonmonotonic**: too much or too little privacy is unsatisfactory; an optimal level is a momentary equilibrium. Beyond intrusion, the framework adds "overachieved" privacy, or isolation.
- **Boundary regulation**: openness–closedness to others shifts with circumstances.
- **Desired versus achieved privacy**: equal means optimum control.
- **Input and output**: control covers inputs from others and outputs from the self to others (hypothesized).

### Framework — eight derivative privacy situations (Figure 1)

Combining desired/achieved privacy with input/output, for a person or group P and another party or stimulation E:

- **Inputs**: Case 1, P wants E's inputs, opens the boundary, E enters; Case 2, P closes the boundary and keeps E out (the traditional successful case); Case 3, E crosses P's closed boundary (intrusion); Case 4, P is open but contact does not materialize (isolation).
- **Outputs**: Cases 5 and 6 parallel 1 and 2 (P successfully initiates or withholds contact); Case 7, P fails to avoid contact with E, e.g., unintentionally reveals information (intrusion); Case 8, P solicits contact and is rebuffed (isolation).

Exchanges can be analyzed as combinations, e.g., optimum exclusion = Cases 2 + 6.

[Example] A mix of Cases 1 and 8: P listens to E's problem and admits E, but P's hope that E will listen in return is rebuffed.

### Framework — privacy mechanisms

Mechanisms operate as an integrated system that substitutes, complements, or conflicts (verbal praise with a hostile glance conveys nongenuineness), and as a feedback system: when one fails, others are mobilized, like a thermostat.

- **Verbal**: content ("Keep out", "I'd like you to listen to me") and structure (Mahl and Schulze; Mehrabian's verbal immediacy, greater toward liked people).
- **Nonverbal**: reactions to spatial intrusion (closer intrusion, more blocking, turning away, flight).
- **Environmental**: personal space (Hall's four zones; liking reduces distance, so boundaries grow more permeable); areas, objects, territories.
- **Cultural**: societies evolve different mixes; Altman agrees with Westin that all societies probably have privacy mechanisms, and says cultures that seem to lack privacy reflect a view of privacy as solely physical.

[Example] The Yagua signify absence by turning toward the wall; a poor Mexican family's parents placed their bed behind a wall of crates; Javanese people use restraint and soft speech.

### Framework — dynamics and costs of regulation

A proposed sequence: a subjective desired level of privacy for the situation, a mix of mechanisms in its service, then match or discrepancy. On discrepancy, people mobilize further behaviors (closing a door, then nonverbal displeasure, verbal requests, removing the intruder, or leaving), or shift the desired level itself, e.g., when an intruder proves likeable. Regulation incurs physical, psychological, and physiological costs, when control is not achieved or is achieved only with great resources. Following Dubos, the issue is the price of adaptation, not survival.

### Framework — functions of privacy

Three proposed functions, building on one another:

- **Interpersonal**: regulating interaction and balancing openness with withholding; success or failure here informs self-definition.
- **Interface of self and social world**: self-evaluation (Westin) — integrating experiences, planning future relations, away from others.
- **Self-identity**: "the ultimate goal of privacy regulation" — knowing where the self begins and ends, its capabilities and limits. The central claim: it is not inclusion or exclusion itself but the *ability* to control boundaries that contributes to self-definition.

## Future Directions

- Privacy dynamics across social units, "an important area of analysis".
- The costs of failing, or straining, to maintain boundaries: "little is known", yet "critical".
- Verbal and nonverbal privacy mechanisms, little studied.
