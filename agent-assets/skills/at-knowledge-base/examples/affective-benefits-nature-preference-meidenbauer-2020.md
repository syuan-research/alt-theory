# The affective benefits of nature exposure: What's nature got to do with it? (Meidenbauer et al., 2020)

## Summary

Nature exposure reliably improves affect, and adults strongly prefer natural over urban scenes, but most studies do not control for preference, so it is unclear whether the benefit comes from nature per se or from viewing a highly preferred stimulus. Across six online studies (Mechanical Turk), the authors built preference-equated image sets of nature, urban, and animal scenes and measured state affect (short STAI) before and after viewing, also testing baseline negative mood, beauty versus liking, and the rating prompt. When preference was matched, environment type made no difference; participants' own preference ratings predicted affect change. The authors conclude that nature improves affect "because it is such a highly preferred environment."

## Aims

- "to test the effects of environment type (nature vs. urban) and aesthetic value (preference level) on changes in affect from pre to post viewing of image sets."
- Pre-registered hypothesis: nature images may generate a larger benefit to state affect than preference-equated urban images.
- Three competing possibilities tested (Study 2, Fig. 1): **A** nature is unique; **B** aesthetic preference is what matters; **C** both matter.

## Key Concepts

### Preference (affinity, "liking") vs. affect

Preference is necessarily directed at an object ("there is no self-contained state of 'preference'"), whereas a change in affective state (along valence and arousal) is a general, physiologically based response that can arise from external stimuli or interoception. Being exposed to a preferred thing can change affect, but affect change need not involve a preferred stimulus; hence "preference and affect are not one in the same."

### Aesthetic value

Operationalized as the mean preference rating (1–7, "strongly dislike" to "strongly like") an image set receives; the paper uses aesthetics, preference, and affinity interchangeably until Study 5 tests whether liking and beauty coincide.

### Nature (operationalization)

Scenes "with primarily natural (non-built) elements," varied in landscape type, without people, animals, or single objects. Study 4 distinguishes **scenic nature** (environments) from **domestic nature** (animal images).

### Competing accounts of nature's benefits (as the paper frames them)

- **Attention Restoration Theory**: cognitive restoration independent of affect change and of aesthetic preference.
- **Stress Reduction Theory**: aesthetic preference drives affective benefits, which in turn free cognitive resources.
- **Perceptual Fluency Account**: affective benefit comes from fluent processing of features such as fractalness, not necessarily beauty.
- Others cited: endogenous visual reward from spatial-frequency properties; Prospect-Refuge; Kuo's "active ingredients."

All reference nature's effects rather than those of a "preferred stimulus," which motivates testing naturalness against preference.

## Main Content

[Context: Online image-viewing experiments, US adults (Amazon Mechanical Turk)]

All participants were US-based MTurk adults; designs were between-subjects to avoid order and anchoring effects across repeated affect ratings. State affect was a 6-item STAI (STAI-Pos: calm, relaxed, content; STAI-Neg: upset, tense, worried) plus visual analog scales; analyses report the STAI composites (PCA results in supplements). Analyses were ANOVAs with Tukey post hoc tests and linear regressions in R. Studies 2, 4, 5 were pre-registered; data and materials are on OSF.

### Study 1 — image validation for preference-equated sets

401 participants rated 100 of 375 images (no people, animals, or text) on preference or naturalness. Six 45-image sets resulted: preference-matched High (≈4.6) and Low (≈3.8) Aesthetic Value Nature/Urban pairs (HA-Nat/HA-Urb, LA-Nat/LA-Urb), plus Very High nature (VHA-Nat, 6.34) and Very Low urban (VLA-Urb, 2.64). Strongly liked urban images and strongly disliked nature images were too few to match the extremes. Naturalness differed sharply (nature ≈6.7, urban ≈1.4).

### Study 2 — affect change by environment and aesthetic value

615 participants were randomly assigned to one of the six sets, rated affect (T1), viewed and rated 45 images (≥7 s each; ≈7 min), then rated affect again (T2).

- VHA-Nat, HA-Nat, and HA-Urb improved affect; the LA sets mostly did not (LA-Nat raised STAI-Pos modestly); VLA-Urb worsened it.
- In the fully crossed HA/LA × Nature/Urban subset, there were no effects of environment, aesthetic value, or their interaction (all p > .1).
- Using participants' own mean ratings, preference predicted STAI-Pos change (B = 0.07), but environment and its interaction did not.
- Within nature, VHA-Nat improved affect more than LA-Nat (d ≈ 0.33); within urban, VLA-Urb produced less improvement than HA-Urb and LA-Urb (d = 0.40–0.50).
- Across all participants, mean preference explained 5.5% of STAI-Pos and 2.4% of STAI-Neg change.

The largest benefit came from VHA-Nat, but it had no equally preferred urban counterpart, which motivated Studies 3–4.

### Study 3 — very-high-preference images in other categories

409 participants rated 75 images each in five categories (nature, urban, animals, space, abstract art). Only urban and animal images overlapped enough with very high nature preference; three 30-image sets were matched (preference ≈5.4 each; naturalness: nature 6.52, animals 6.47, urban 2.73).

### Study 4 — very highly preferred images with mood induction

602 participants were assigned to image category (nature, urban, animals) × mood induction (negative story about a father's death from Alzheimer's vs. neutral excerpt from *A Brief History of Time*). The induction addressed the concern that Study 2's high baseline positive affect left little room to change.

- Negative induction produced much larger affect improvement (STAI-Pos ηp² = .21; STAI-Neg ηp² = .30).
- Image category had no effect and did not interact with mood induction.
- Preference ratings diverged from Study 3 (urban 5.16, nature 5.69, animals 5.89), so regressions included own preference: preference predicted greater STAI-Pos increase (B = 0.14) and STAI-Neg reduction (B = −0.09), while category did not.

Very highly preferred nature therefore improved affect no more than equally preferred urban or animal images.

### Study 5 — beauty vs. liking

To test whether nature is liked for something beyond beauty (e.g., endogenous visual reward), 194 new participants rated all 375 Study 1 images on beauty ("How ugly or beautiful is this image?"). Beauty and Study 1 liking correlated r = .97 overall (nature .96, urban .95). The authors find no evidence of a nature-specific factor making nature liked beyond aesthetics.

### Study 6 — replication with naturalness ratings

Because rating preference during viewing might have primed preference, 607 participants repeated Study 2 but rated naturalness instead. Naturalness ratings shifted when only one environment type was seen (urban mean 2.75 vs. 1.42; over a quarter rated city images as more natural than man-made), so analyses used categorical factors only. Results largely replicated Study 2: no environment effect in the crossed subset, VHA-Nat > LA-Nat for positive affect, VLA-Urb worse than HA-Urb for negative affect. No condition reduced negative affect, which the authors attribute to high baseline negative affect (data collected June 1, 2020, amid the Covid-19 pandemic and protests against police brutality).

### Results and Interpretations — a preference-based account

Across studies, nature improved affect because it was highly preferred; matched-preference urban images, and very highly preferred urban or animal images, produced the same benefit. The authors stress that this does not make the nature category meaningless: finding urban images as liked as nature was very hard (none matched VHA-Nat in Study 1; only two of four categories matched in Study 3), so many prior nature-vs-urban comparisons likely used unequally preferred stimuli (e.g., ≈5.5 vs. ≈2.8 in Berman et al., 2008). Nature is "special" in being so overwhelmingly preferred. The study does not explain why nature is preferred. For the ART–SRT debate, SRT would predict no cognitive advantage of nature over equally preferred urban settings, whereas ART does not tie restoration to preference.

**Selected limitations the authors note:** images cannot capture other sensory qualities or longer exposure, and preference-equated real environments may be nearly impossible to find; other affective outcomes such as awe and compassion were not measured; there was no equally disliked nature condition, so urban detriments remain open.

## Future Directions

- A strong test of preference's role in cognitive benefits: compare objective cognitive performance before and after preference-equated nature and urban images, videos, or walks.
- Examine other evaluative judgments (e.g., restorativeness, pleasantness) that might diverge between scene types.
- Examine other affective benefits of nature, such as awe and compassion.
- Test whether equally un-preferred nature produces detriments like those of very low aesthetic urban scenes.

## Omissions

- Full statistics for every analysis, including per-condition t-tests (Tables 3, 8) and regression tables (Tables 6A–6B).
- PCA-based and pre-registered analyses reported in the supplementary materials.
- Image selection criteria and viewpoint variation in detail, and per-study compensation and timing.
- Baseline affect levels and VAS emotion results.
- The paper's discussion of origins of nature preference (biophilia, restorative value) and of biophilic design features (fractalness, curved edges, color properties).
