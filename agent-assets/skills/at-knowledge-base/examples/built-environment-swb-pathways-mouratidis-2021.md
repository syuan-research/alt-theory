# Urban planning and quality of life: A review of pathways linking the built environment to subjective well-being (Mouratidis, 2021)

## Summary

Links between the built environment and subjective well-being (SWB) are not sufficiently understood, and earlier conceptualizations each capture only some of the relevant pathways, with some pathways ignored. The paper proposes a conceptual model organizing these links into seven pathways — travel, leisure, work, social relationships, residential well-being, emotional responses, health — and uses it to structure a qualitative, non-exhaustive review of about 150 mostly peer-reviewed studies. It synthesizes evidence on each pathway, for example that denser urban form goes with weaker neighbor ties but larger overall social networks, and derives a set of urban planning strategies for improving SWB.

## Objectives

"(1) to present a conceptual model that organizes the pathways linking the built environment to SWB, (2) to provide an overview of the empirical evidence on these pathways, and (3) based on the knowledge from the overview, to present potential strategies on how to improve SWB through urban planning."

## Key Concepts

### Subjective well-being (SWB)

"The personal evaluation of quality of life" (Diener, Oishi, & Tay, 2018), comprising life satisfaction (contentment with life overall), emotional well-being (affect, hedonic well-being), and eudaimonia (self-actualization and meaning in life).

### Built environment

"The physical human-made environment where human activity occurs," organized here into four components: land use, transport systems, urban design, and housing.

### Pathways as life domains

The model's pathways "mostly correspond to life domains," all of which contribute to SWB. Domains influence one another through **spill-over effects** (Sirgy, 2012), so all pathways are interlinked.

### Residential well-being

After Sirgy (2012): "residents' attitude toward their living space," "feelings of gratification from living in a specific space," "residents' perceptions of quality of life of their community," evaluated at three scales: housing, neighborhood, and city satisfaction.

### Compact urban form

Used throughout for high-density, mixed-use, centrally located environments with short distances, walkability, and public transport, contrasted with low-density, car-oriented sprawl or suburbs.

## Main Content

### Method — qualitative synthesis

[Context: Urban planning; mainly economically developed countries]

A synthesis of literature for an overview of the state of knowledge, not an exhaustive review of each pathway. About 150 studies, mostly peer-reviewed articles in international journals, with a few books, chapters, and reports. Search: relevant review papers and highly relevant empirical papers, backward snowballing, supplemented by database searches; several relevant papers were excluded to keep the literature manageable.

### Framework — model of pathways linking the built environment to SWB (proposed)

Developed analytically by synthesizing earlier frameworks (Marans, 2003; Mouratidis, 2018) and recent empirical findings; the organization of seven pathways is presented as novel.

- **Components:** the built environment (land use, transport systems, urban design, housing) → seven pathways (travel, leisure, work, social relationships, residential well-being, emotional responses, health) → SWB.
- **Relations:** pathways are mediating, parallel, and interlinked through spill-over; interconnections are omitted from the figure to reduce complexity. The reverse link from SWB to domains exists but is not examined.
- **Delimitation:** sociodemographics, personality traits, and values are treated as moderators, not mediating pathways. Domains such as civic duties and rights, spirituality, and religion are excluded for lack of clear links and adequate evidence.
- **Health** is bidirectionally linked to SWB and to most other pathways; it could be a final outcome, but here it is examined for its contribution to SWB.
- **Stated use:** theoretical and methodological guidance for empirical research, and structure for the review and the strategies.

### Synthesis — travel

Travel can affect all SWB components through four routes: (1) access to places, facilities, and services, enabling needs fulfillment and eudaimonia; (2) emotional responses to trips; (3) physical activity or its lack; (4) spill-over to leisure, work, and neighborhood satisfaction. Short travel times and active modes raise travel satisfaction; walking and cycling are the most pleasant modes, car driving the least pleasant and most stressful "at least in certain cases." Density, proximity to the center, mixed uses, walkability, and transit promote active travel; long commutes increase stress and lower leisure and job satisfaction. ICT and new mobility options may improve inclusiveness; COVID-19 boosted online activities with possible post-pandemic implications for SWB.

### Synthesis — leisure

Leisure satisfaction is shaped by participation in social, physical, and preferred activities, by constraints, and by available time; longer commutes reduce it. Direct studies are scarce: in one tested model, urban greenness and local amenities related positively to leisure satisfaction, while compact form related negatively via less green space and positively via amenities, shorter commutes, and social interaction. Green space gained importance during COVID-19.

### Synthesis — work

Cities provide work and education opportunities; denser cities are argued to increase access and economic mobility, but under neoliberal organization can also be arenas of inequality. Long commutes lower job satisfaction, and commute satisfaction contributes to SWB partly through job satisfaction. Telework can reduce commute costs but may encourage urban expansion.

### Synthesis — social relationships

Probably the most important SWB domain. The built environment shapes ties through spatial propinquity, composition, and configuration.

- **Local ties:** easy access to destinations, good walking conditions, and green communal spaces relate to stronger neighborhood cohesion; density and numerous local amenities relate to lower cohesion. Proposed explanations: low-density housing types allow more meaningful neighbor interaction; lower density gives more control over whom one meets; central residents can more easily maintain ties elsewhere and need neighbors less. Local ties matter more for vulnerable groups such as older adults and people in poverty.
- **Overall ties:** compact-city residents tend to have more close relationships, socialize more often, receive stronger support, and are happier with their personal relationships than suburban residents, "although they may not even know their neighbors."

### Synthesis — residential well-being

- **Housing satisfaction** relates to life satisfaction, happiness, and eudaimonia; correlates include plan, design, size, interior space, construction quality, amenities, price, ownership, and communal spaces.
- **Neighborhood satisfaction:** objective correlates are location, access to local amenities, and usable green space; perceived correlates (which correlate more strongly) include attachment, accessibility, social cohesion, safety, public space and aesthetic quality, and quietness. Deprivation lowers it. A compact environment can outperform sprawl when noise, inequality, crime, and lack of green space are limited.
- **City satisfaction:** dissatisfaction with public transport, cultural facilities, retail, green space, air quality, trust, and administration contributes to dissatisfaction with city life; safety raises it; lower social inequality relates to higher quality of life.

### Synthesis — emotional responses

Well-maintained vegetation, upkeep and order, and openness trigger positive emotions; green space reduces stress and mental fatigue and supports attention recovery and safety. Physical disorder raises perceived social disorder and lowers perceived safety (Broken Windows Theory); routine activities raise perceived safety. Aesthetically pleasing public spaces and architecture relate to momentary happiness, and contemporary architecture scores lower than traditional in environmental perception. Lower momentary happiness in cities than in natural or rural settings, and more positive emotions in suburbs than inner cities, may indicate a negative link between dense, vibrant surroundings and emotional well-being.

### Synthesis — health

The built environment affects health through the other six pathways and through routes they do not capture, such as air pollution (compact form linked to higher PM2.5 in a Chinese study). Urban nature reduces noise, air pollution, and heat stress and relates to better mental health and life satisfaction. Urbanity is linked to schizophrenia risk, stress, and anxiety, possibly via poverty, inequality, noise, and loss of nature, but also to lower depression risk. Urban residents live longer on average than rural ones, possibly only the privileged; compact development relates to higher life expectancy. An overview of systematic reviews found insufficient evidence on causal links between the built environment and mental health.

### Practice — strategies for improving SWB through urban planning (Table 1)

Derived from the pathway review and presented as far from complete, with benefits depending on individuals and context, and most relevant to economically developed countries.

- **Urban nature:** gardens, parks, street trees, green roofs, access to nearby nature.
- **Public and communal spaces:** accessible, inclusive public spaces; high-quality communal spaces for residential complexes.
- **Facilities and services:** easy, equitable access.
- **Active travel and public transport:** improve transit, walkability, cyclability; pedestrianization; restrict cars and parking where possible.
- **Technology and emerging mobility:** steer them toward inclusiveness.
- **Upkeep and order** of spaces, vegetation, and transport systems.
- **Noise reduction:** restrict noisy vehicles and activities, reduce speeds, improve insulation.
- **Aesthetic quality** based on residents' needs and preferences.
- **Socio-spatial equity:** support housing and transport for vulnerable groups.
- **Planning processes:** evidence-based policies and laws; planner–public health knowledge transfer; measurement and benchmarking; empowerment and public participation, with the caution that participation can privilege self-interest and suits poorly some urgent or technical matters.

## Future Directions

- Use the model and review as theoretical and methodological guidance for empirical research.
- Explore built environment–SWB links during COVID-19 when the evidence is more mature, and re-evaluate them in post-pandemic times.
- Elaborate the strategies into more detailed actions.
