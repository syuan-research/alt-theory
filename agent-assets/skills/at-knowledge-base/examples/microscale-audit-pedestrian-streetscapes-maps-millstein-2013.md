## Summary

Streetscape (microscale) features may influence physical activity, and many audit tools exist, but few have published, systematically derived scoring methods that link to policy. The paper presents the MAPS audit tool (adapted from earlier tools), a theory-, expert- and policy-based scoring system that groups items into hierarchical subscales, and the inter-rater reliability of items and scores from two independent raters in three US regions. Of 201 items, 160 entered subscales; most showed moderate to excellent reliability. Validity against physical activity is not tested.

## Aims

"We present the development, scoring, and reliability of the Microscale Audit of Pedestrian Streetscapes (MAPS) tool and its theoretically-based subscales." Overall goal: summary scores that assess detailed built-environment attributes relevant to physical activity, for research, city planning and community advocacy.

[Example] Scores could guide city planning and transport decisions and help residents see strengths and weaknesses of local streetscapes.

## Key Concepts

- **Microscale factors**: streets, sidewalks, intersections and design details (crossing features, trees, bicycle lanes, curbs) and social-environment cues (stray dogs, graffiti, trash), as against macro-level attributes (walkability, density, connectivity, land-use mix).
- **Valence score**: sum of subscales expected to help (positive) or hinder (negative) physical activity; negative is subtracted from positive to give section scores.
- **Route, segment, crossing, cul-de-sac**: the four audit sections (see Method — MAPS tool).

## Study — MAPS development, scoring and inter-rater reliability

[Context: pedestrian streetscapes, physical activity, children/teens/older adults]

### Method — data source and routes

- Three parent studies of neighborhood design and physical activity (children 6–11, teens 12–16, seniors 65–97) in San Diego County, Seattle/King County and Baltimore-DC; neighborhoods varied on a GIS walkability index, income or food/recreation environment. Collected 2009–2010; 2117 routes.
- Each route is about 0.25 mile from a participant's home toward the nearest eligible destination (cluster of 3+ commercial locations, park or school), found with ArcGIS Network Analyst and Google Maps, following the road network. The authors note the quarter-mile standard may not suit all purposes.

### Instrument — MAPS tool (adapted, not newly invented)

Adapted mainly from the Analytic Audit Tool, with items added for seniors or youth (for example sidewalk cross-slope) and a cul-de-sac section for youth studies. Four sections:
- **Route**: land use and destinations, transit stops, amenities, traffic calming, aesthetics, social environment.
- **Segment** (street between crossings; up to 8 per route): sidewalks, buffers, slope, bicycle facilities, building aesthetics, trees, setbacks.
- **Crossing** (up to 5): crosswalks, slopes, widths, signals, pedestrian protection.
- **Cul-de-sac** (within 400 feet of home): surface, slope, surveillance, amenities.

### Procedure — rater training and reliability sample

- Raters had 3 days of training; certification required at least four routes with at least 95% inter-rater agreement. Mostly paper forms.
- Two certified raters independently coded 13.7% of routes, the second within a week: 290 route pairs, 516 segment pairs, 319 crossing pairs, 53 cul-de-sac pairs. Residential routes took 28.5 minutes on average.

### Method — scoring system (proposed by this paper)

- An a priori framework drew on literature and expert opinion (safety, aesthetics, destinations, land use, recreation, transport, social environment). Decisions rested on team consensus, theory, and policy relevance (different agencies control land use, streetscape and aesthetics).
- Items were sorted into subscales a priori. Dichotomous items scored 0/1; frequency items (0, 1, 2+) scored 0, 1, 2; continuous items were dichotomized or trichotomized; related items were combined. Slope items were scored separately for seniors.
- Hierarchy: items to subscales, positive and negative valence scores (sums), section scores (positive minus negative), and a route overall score as the sum of three route subsections (destinations and land use, streetscape, aesthetics and social). Segment and crossing sections have overall scores; cul-de-sacs one positive score. Totals can be summed across sections.
- Item reliability: Kappa (dichotomous), one-way random single-measure ICC (continuous/ordinal) and percent agreement. Good/excellent: Kappa or ICC at least .60, agreement at least 75%; moderate .41–.60, 60–74%; poor at most .40, under 60%. An item was acceptable if either statistic was moderate or higher. Poor items were dropped unless experts judged them necessary; subscales were acceptable at ICC moderate or higher. Analysis in SPSS 17.0.

### Results — items and subscales

- 41 of 201 items (20.4%) were dropped (low reliability, low frequency, text responses, or no consensus on expected direction). Of 160 included: 80 (50.0%) good/excellent, 41 (25.6%) moderate, 18 (11.3%) low, 21 (13.1%) too rare to compute.
- **Route**: 10 positive and 1 negative land-use subscales; streetscape and aesthetics/social subsections; route overall ICC .816. Aesthetics and social scores were the weakest (negative .514, subsection .580).
- **Segment**: 7 positive and 2 negative subscales; building height and setbacks poor (ICC .370); overall .753 (children/teens), .758 (seniors).
- **Crossing**: 3 positive and 2 negative subscales; overall ICC .830; negative valence score .587.
- **Cul-de-sac**: 12 items, one subscale, ICC .847.
- Overall: 17 of 20 route-section scores, 12 of 16 segment and 6 of 8 crossing scores good/excellent; the rest mostly moderate.

### Limitations and failure conditions (selected)

- Scoring choices mixed theory, practicality and consensus and were revised during data reduction; subtracting negatives from positives may not capture complexity.
- The route may not match a participant's actual route, and key features may lie beyond the quarter mile.
- Scoring may not generalize to other purposes (for example social capital) or contexts (Asian or African cities).

## Future Directions

Validity analyses relating scores to physical activity and walking outcomes; an electronic data-collection interface; a shorter form of the most valid items.
