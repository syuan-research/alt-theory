# Participation among adults with disability: The role of the urban environment (Clarke et al., 2011)

## Summary

The ICF treats participation as shaped by environmental factors, but how specific built-environment features interact with impairments and activity limitations to affect participation had received little empirical attention. Using the Chicago Community Adult Health Study (adults 45+, N = 1225), the authors fit logistic models of three participation indicators — interpersonal interaction, preventive health care, voting — testing impairment × environment interactions at block group and census tract scale, with environment measured by systematic social observation. Residential security raised interaction among adults with walking difficulty, heavy traffic lowered preventive care among adults with visual impairment, and poor streets lowered voting among adults with mobility difficulty; the salient spatial scale differed by outcome.

## Aims

- "to examine the role of certain characteristics in the urban built environment as they interact with underlying impairments and activity limitations either to promote or hinder the participation of adults in society."
- Hypotheses: heavy traffic, poor streets, low residential security, lack of transit, and social disorder impede participation among people with visual or hearing impairment or movement-related limitations; interaction is more sensitive to the proximate block group (~1500 people), voting to the larger tract (~4000 people).

## Key Concepts

### Disability and ICF levels of functioning

Disability is an umbrella term "encompassing impairments, activity limitations and participation restrictions, which are all dynamically influenced by environmental and individual factors" (WHO, 2001). The ICF classifies functioning at body, individual (activities), and society (participation) levels; environmental and personal factors can impede or enhance each. The paper models the built environment as a modifier of the pathway from impairments/activity limitations to participation.

### Participation

"Involvement in a life situation" (WHO, 2001): performance at the societal level through social roles, as distinct from individual-level activity. The paper does not address how to measure participation as a construct.

### Systematic social observation (SSO)

Interviewers rate the respondent's residential block with a standardized instrument; block-face ratings are aggregated to block group and tract via multilevel item response models.

## Main Content

### Participants and Data — Chicago Community Adult Health Study

[Context: Urban neighborhoods, Chicago, USA]

2002 face-to-face interviews with a representative sample of 3105 Chicago adults in 343 neighborhood clusters (response rate 72%); analyses use the 1225 aged 45+. Mean age 60; 42.7% White, 35.8% Black, 18.5% Hispanic. One fifth reported difficulty walking 2–3 blocks, 10% difficulty with other mobility activities, 36% some visual difficulty.

### Instrument — measures

- **Built environment (SSO, standardized):** percent of streets in very poor condition (cracks, potholes, broken curbs); percent with heavy traffic; transit line within 500 m; physical and social disorder (Raudenbush & Sampson, 1999; α = .94); residential security from crime-watch and security signs (α = .72).
- **Participation (binary):** meeting friends, neighbors, or relatives at least monthly; physical exam plus blood pressure and cholesterol checks in two years; voting in the last federal or mayoral election.
- **Impairments/limitations (self-report):** visual and hearing indices; any difficulty walking 2–3 blocks; marked difficulty with seven mobility activities plus heavy housework.
- **Controls and candidate mediators:** sociodemographics, health, cognition, insurance; fear of walking at night, perceived violence, traffic danger, noise, air quality, physical hazards.

### Method — logistic models at two spatial scales

Weighted binary logistic regression per outcome, single-level at block group and multilevel at tract, adding impairment × environment interactions, then controls, then mediators.

### Results and Interpretations — interpersonal interaction

Effects appeared only at block group level. The one significant interaction was walking difficulty × residential security: security had no effect for adults without walking difficulty, but for those with it, adjusted predicted odds of interaction were 45% higher in high- than low-security areas. Fear of walking predicted less interaction (OR = .77) but did not mediate; the authors point to unmeasured elements such as street lighting or police presence. Heavy traffic was associated with more interaction, possibly via unmeasured transit stops or cafes.

### Results and Interpretations — preventive health care

Again block group only. Visual impairment lowered the odds of care (OR = .74), moderated by traffic: no traffic association without visual impairment, but with controls the odds fell by 20% (mild) and 50% (severe impairment) per SD of heavy traffic. In light-traffic areas, adults with visual impairment had odds similar to those without. Social disorder's association disappeared with controls (read as neighborhood composition). Perceived traffic danger, noise, and air quality did not mediate.

### Results and Interpretations — voting

Effects appeared at tract level; 15% of voting variance lay between tracts (ICC = .153). For adults with mobility difficulty, poor streets lowered the odds of voting (adjusted OR = .51 per SD), with predicted odds almost 60% lower in poor- than good-street tracts. Good streets acted as a "leveler": voting odds were similar with and without mobility difficulty. Perceived hazards did not mediate.

### Results and Interpretations — spatial scale

Interaction and health care responded to the immediate block group, voting to the larger tract; the authors conclude that the definition of "neighborhood" matters.

**Selected limitations:** no data on voting mode (mail voters may be less affected) or on impairment duration, which may attenuate associations if participation predated onset.

## Future Directions

- Investigate how heavy traffic acts as a barrier to health care for people with visual impairment.
- Further ICF-framed studies in other built and social environments.
